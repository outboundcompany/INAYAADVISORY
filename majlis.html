What the Majlis is

In the Gulf, a majlis is a seated council where people come to know one another before business is discussed.

  1. 01

    A small standing group

    Family offices, institutional allocators, fund managers and principals. The group is kept small so that every member can take part.

  2. 02

    Seats by selection

    Membership is not open. We review each request and offer a seat where interests align.

  3. 03

    Held in confidence

    Members are not named, and what is discussed is neither published nor attributed.

  4. 04

    Hosted with David Hunter

    Former Executive Director and Chief Investment Officer of the Texas Municipal Retirement System and the North Dakota Retirement and Investment Office.

The allocators

Behind every allocator are the people it invests for.

A firefighter's pension, a university's scholarships, a family's savings and a country's reserves all reach private markets through an allocator. Each one decides in its own way, and the Majlis brings the people who make those decisions into one room.

  • Institutions. Pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments and foundations, investing over long horizons through a committee.
  • Families and wealth. Family offices and private wealth, investing in funds and directly in companies.
  • Gatekeepers. Consultants and funds of funds, who decide which managers many institutions see.
01Institutions

Pension Funds

Public and corporate plans invest to pay the retirement benefits of their members, through a formal process a manager needs to understand before the first meeting.

  • Approved in stages. The board sets a target, staff run diligence, a consultant often recommends and the board approves.
  • The same questions. Fit with the allocation, who made the returns and whether reporting meets a public plan's standard.
02Institutions

Sovereign Wealth Funds

Sovereign funds invest national reserves for a state and its citizens, often across generations. Several of the most active in private markets are in the Gulf.

  • Scale and capacity. How much capital the strategy can take, and how a large commitment would sit in the fund.
  • Co-investment and the long term. Direct opportunities alongside a fund, and a manager to back across several funds.
03Institutions

Endowments and Foundations

Endowments invest to support universities, schools and hospitals. Foundations invest so they can keep funding their charitable work.

  • Spending and liquidity. Commitments are sized against the annual spending need, so the pace of distributions matters.
  • Long relationships. Many back managers early and stay through several funds, so succession is read closely.
04Families and wealth

Family Offices

A family office manages the wealth of one family or of several. It can decide faster than an institution and often invests in funds and directly in companies.

  • The principals. The family members who built the wealth often still take part in decisions.
  • Trusted introductions. Most new managers reach a family office through someone it trusts.
05Families and wealth

Private Wealth

Advisers, private banks and principals who invest for individuals and families, asking different questions from an institution on behalf of a client.

  • Access and minimums. Whether clients can invest, at what size, and whether a feeder is available.
  • Liquidity and reporting. When a client can get money back, and whether tax documents arrive on time.
06Gatekeepers

Consultants and Funds of Funds

Consultants advise institutions on which managers to back, and funds of funds invest on their behalf. Together they shape which managers many institutions see.

  • Process and repeatability. A process that is written down, applied consistently and owned by more than one person.
  • Terms against peers. Fund terms measured against a peer group the consultant already covers.
