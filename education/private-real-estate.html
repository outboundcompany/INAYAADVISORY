How is private real estate capital raised?

Private real estate capital follows managers who can show income, control and a credible exit, asset by asset.

Real estate allocators underwrite buildings before they underwrite people. A committee will read the rent roll, the lease expiry profile, the debt schedule and the exit cap rate before it spends long on the track record summary. Managers who raise well present each strategy, from core through opportunistic and ground-up development, as a set of asset business plans that an investor can test line by line.

Strategy labels carry precise expectations. Core means stabilised, well let, modestly geared property held for income. Core-plus adds light lease-up or refurbishment. Value-add buys vacancy, capital expenditure or repositioning. Opportunistic and development take entitlement, construction and leasing risk for a higher return target. A manager whose gearing, holding period or return target does not match the label it uses will lose credibility in the first meeting.

How a real estate raise runs

  1. Strategy and pipeline

    Define sector, geography, risk band and gearing policy, supported by identified or seed assets under control.

  2. Materials and room

    Prepare deck, track record by deal, asset business plans, rent rolls, valuations and debt schedules.

  3. Anchor, first close

    Secure an anchor with economics or rights, then hold first close so acquisitions can begin.

  4. Subsequent closes

    Add investors over twelve to eighteen months, equalising later entrants and showing deployment against plan.

  5. Deploy and report

    Acquire, execute business plans, report NAV, NOI and leasing quarterly, then prepare the next vintage.

Metrics real estate committees test

Realized vs unrealized

Returns from assets sold and cash distributed, set apart from returns still resting on appraised values.

What allocators look for

A meaningful realized record by vintage, and unrealized marks that hold up against recent comparable sales.

Gross to net spread

The gap between asset-level returns and investor returns after fees, promote, fund costs and cash drag.

What allocators look for

A spread explained line by line, and net figures shown alongside gross in every track record table.

Entry and exit cap

Purchase price expressed as a yield on NOI, and the yield assumed when the asset is sold.

What allocators look for

Exit caps at or above entry caps unless the business plan clearly changes the asset quality.

NOI growth

Change in net operating income from rents, occupancy and expense control after property operating costs.

What allocators look for

Growth tied to signed leases, market rent evidence and capital spend, with cap rate compression treated as upside.

What diligence covers

01

Asset business plans

Allocators rebuild the underwriting for each asset: purchase price, capital expenditure budget, lease-up timetable, rent growth, hold period and exit cap. They compare the plan at acquisition with what happened, and they look for a manager who explains variances openly rather than reworking the base case after the event.

02

Track record attribution

Committees want returns by deal, vintage, sector and strategy, split between income, NOI growth and cap rate movement. A record built mostly on yield compression in a falling rate period is treated with caution. Attribution that shows operating gains survives the questioning far better.

03

Debt and hedging

Diligence teams read every loan agreement summary: lender, rate basis, hedge terms, maturity, extension options, covenants and recourse. They test refinancing at current rates and lower values. Concentrated maturities, unhedged floating debt or cross-collateralised facilities without a clear rationale slow committees down considerably.
