What an investment committee asks

Six questions an institutional allocator puts to a fund manager, and what sits behind each.

Questions that come up in most committee reviews of a fund manager

6

What does a committee ask?

An institutional allocator does not commit on a meeting alone.

Before a fund reaches its investment committee, the staff presenting it will need answers to a familiar set of questions.

What are the six questions?

The order and the emphasis vary from one allocator to the next. The questions themselves change very little.

  1. 01
    Who makes the decisions, and will they still be here?Team and continuity
  2. 02
    What has been achieved, and by whom?Track record and attribution
  3. 03
    Why this strategy, why this team, and why now?Strategy and edge
  4. 04
    Do the terms put the manager and the investor on the same side?Fund terms and alignment
  5. 05
    What will the capital be invested in first?Pipeline
  6. 06
    Will the fund be run and reported to institutional standard?Operations and reporting

Who makes the decisions, and what have they achieved?

The first two questions are about the people in the room, and whether the record belongs to them.

Team and continuity

Committees look at who sits on the investment committee of the manager, how long the senior team has worked together, and who owns the management company. They read key person provisions closely, and they ask how carried interest is shared, since that tends to decide whether a team stays together for the life of the fund.

Track record and attribution

Allocators separate realised results from unrealised marks and ask for deal-level data alongside fund totals. They then ask which of the people now in the room were responsible for each result. A record built by a team that has since changed is weighed accordingly.

Why this strategy, and on what terms?

The committee tests where the deals come from, and whether the terms put the manager and the investor on the same side.

Strategy and edge

The committee wants to understand where the deals come from and why the manager can win them at a sensible price. It also checks that past investments match the strategy now being described. Drift between the two raises questions that are difficult to answer late in a process.

Fund terms and alignment

Management fees, carried interest, the hurdle and the commitment of the general partner are compared against the market for that strategy. Fund size is tested against the opportunity, and governance, including the role of an advisory committee, is read for how conflicts will be handled.

What will the capital do, and how will the fund be run?

The last two questions are about the early investments and the operations behind the fund, since reporting is how the relationship is conducted once the capital is committed.

Pipeline

Allocators ask to see the transactions the manager expects to complete in the early part of the investment period, and how far along they are. A credible pipeline shows that the strategy can be deployed at the pace the fund terms assume.

Operations and reporting

Operational diligence covers the administrator, the auditor, the valuation policy, compliance and cyber security. Committees also ask what investors will receive, how often, and in what format, since reporting is how the relationship is conducted once the capital is committed.

Notes and sources

  1. [1] The questions above reflect general practice among institutional investors and vary between allocators.
