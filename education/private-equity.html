Introduction to private equity

What you need to know

  1. 01

    Structure

    Closed-ended fund, or a vehicle for a single transaction.

  2. 02

    Capital

    Committed at the start, drawn down as investments are made.

  3. 03

    Investors

    Pensions, endowments, sovereign funds, family offices, private wealth.

  4. 04

    Timing

    A first close, then later closes up to a final close.

How is private equity capital raised?

Private equity capital is committed to a manager's judgment over a decade, so allocators underwrite the people first.

A private equity commitment is a promise of capital that is drawn over several years and returned only as companies are sold. Limited partners therefore assess the sponsor as much as the strategy: how deals are sourced, how value is created after closing, and whether earlier funds have converted paper gains into cash. Most committees read the track record deal by deal before they read the deck.

Distributions have run below historical norms for several years, and many limited partners still hold large unrealized positions from earlier vintages. That has changed the order of questions in a fundraise. Allocators now ask about DPI, exit timing and holding periods early, and they compare a sponsor's realized outcomes against vintage peers and public market equivalents before they discuss a new commitment.

How a private equity fund unfolds

  1. Pre-marketing

    Soft soundings with existing and target LPs to test strategy, size and anchor interest.

  2. First close

    Anchor and early investors sign; investment period begins and capital calls can start.

  3. Subsequent closes

    Later investors join over the marketing window, usually paying an equalisation amount to earlier LPs.

  4. Investment period

    Typically the first several years; new platform deals are made and follow-on reserves set.

  5. Harvest and extension

    Exits return capital; extensions or a continuation vehicle handle assets still held at term.

The numbers allocators test first

DPI

Distributions to paid-in capital: cash actually returned to LPs divided by capital they have contributed to the fund.

What allocators look for

Realized cash on earlier funds, measured against vintage peers, before any weight is given to unrealized marks.

TVPI

Total value to paid-in: distributions plus remaining net asset value, divided by paid-in capital, after fees and carry.

What allocators look for

How much of the multiple is still unrealized, and whether marks are supported by recent transactions or comparables.

Net IRR

Annualised return to LPs after fees, expenses and carried interest, driven by both size and timing of cash flows.

What allocators look for

Whether the figure depends on subscription line timing or a single early exit rather than repeatable outcomes.

Loss ratio

Share of invested capital in deals realized or held below cost, often shown by deal count and by dollars.

What allocators look for

Low capital loss across funds, honest disclosure of write-downs, and what the team changed after its losses.

What diligence covers

01

Track record and attribution

Allocators rebuild the record from deal-level cash flows and ask who led each investment. They check that partners claiming a deal sat on its board and that results from a prior firm are portable, with consent to use them and supporting documents for each figure.

02

Sourcing and deal pipeline

Committees look at where past deals came from: proprietary relationships, intermediated auctions or founder introductions. They review the current pipeline, entry multiples paid relative to peers, and whether the strategy still finds deals of the right size as the fund grows.

03

Operating model

LPs test how value is created after closing. They ask for the hundred-day plan template, the role of operating partners, how management teams are hired or replaced, and evidence from realized deals that margin and revenue gains came from the sponsor's own work.
