How is infrastructure capital raised?

Infrastructure capital rewards managers who can show where the cash flow comes from and who stands behind it.

Private infrastructure covers the physical systems an economy cannot do without: toll roads, airports, ports, water and power networks, fibre, towers, data centres and generation. Allocators buy it for long-dated cash flow that is fixed by contract, set by a regulator or tied to essential usage. The strategy label, from core to opportunistic, tells a committee how much of that cash flow already exists and how much the sponsor still has to build.

Core and core-plus funds own operating, mostly brownfield assets with availability payments, regulated returns or long offtake contracts, and target yield. Value-add and opportunistic funds take construction, ramp-up, merchant or repositioning risk, and target capital growth. Platform strategies sit across that spectrum: a manager backs a development team in renewables, digital infrastructure or transport and grows a pipeline of assets inside one company, often with a long hold in mind.

How an infrastructure raise moves

  1. Strategy and pipeline

    Define the risk band, sectors and geographies; show seed assets or a named pipeline with contract status.

  2. Anchor commitment

    Secure one or two anchors, often with fee breaks or co-investment rights, to reach first close.

  3. Subsequent closes

    Hold several closes over the marketing period, with later investors paying equalisation to earlier ones.

  4. Deploy and operate

    Acquire or build assets, finance at project level, and manage construction, ramp-up and contract renewals.

  5. Hold or exit

    Sell to core buyers, list, recapitalise, or transfer to a continuation vehicle for a longer hold.

Metrics infrastructure committees test

Contracted or regulated share

Proportion of forecast revenue fixed by concession, offtake, availability payment or a regulated asset base, as opposed to volume or price exposure.

What allocators look for

A high, documented share for core claims, with remaining contract life shown year by year.

Cash yield

Distributions paid from operating cash flow as a share of invested capital or net asset value, before any sale proceeds.

What allocators look for

Steady, covered distributions funded by asset cash flow, never by refinancing proceeds or asset sales.

Inflation linkage and pass-through

How far revenue indexes to CPI or similar measures through tariffs, concession terms or regulated allowances, and how costs move against it.

What allocators look for

Pass-through written into contracts or regulation, with caps, lags and cost indexation mismatches disclosed.

Counterparty credit quality

Credit standing of whoever pays: government grantors, utilities, offtakers, hyperscale tenants or carriers under the revenue contracts.

What allocators look for

Investment-grade or sovereign-backed payers, concentration limits, and step-in or termination compensation provisions.

What diligence covers

01

Revenue contracts and counterparties

Allocators read the concession agreement, power purchase or offtake agreements, tenancy contracts and availability mechanisms themselves. They test termination triggers, compensation on early termination, change-in-law protection, indexation formulae and counterparty concentration, and they map every revenue line in the model back to the clause that supports it.

02

Technical and construction review

A technical adviser report covers asset condition, remaining useful life and lifecycle capex, and for greenfield assets the EPC contract, contractor credit, schedule float and performance guarantees. Committees compare the adviser's view with the manager's budget and want to see how past build programs performed against original estimates.

03

Regulatory and grantor position

For regulated utilities and networks, diligence covers the price control framework, the next reset date, allowed return mechanics, capex and opex allowances and penalty regimes. For concessions, it covers the grantor's history of honouring terms. Political and tariff affordability risk is assessed country by country.
