Fewer, larger funds: what concentration means for first-time and mid-size managers

Private capital raised is holding up in total, while the number of funds that close has fallen sharply and the largest funds take most of the money. For a first-time or mid-size manager, that changes how long a raise takes and what each allocator needs to see before it commits.

The total amount of capital committed to private funds has held up better than the number of funds closing. The difference between those two figures is the most important fact for a manager raising today. More of the money is going to fewer managers, and most of it is going to the largest funds.

What the figures show

PitchBook's report on the first half of 2026 counts US$658.1 billion raised across 1,499 private capital funds globally. Funds larger than US$1 billion took 78.2% of that capital, up from 59.1% in 2021. In private equity alone, PitchBook reports that 77% of capital raised since 2024 has gone to funds over US$1 billion, compared with 63.6% from 2014 through 2016.

The count of funds tells the other half of the story. PitchBook's Q3 2026 First Look, as summarised by Foley & Lardner, shows US$368.1 billion of global private equity capital raised in the first nine months of the year, pacing up 3.1%. Over the same period, the number of private equity funds closed was 523, pacing down 30.5%. In the United States, 348 funds closed, pacing down 31.5%, and capital raised was US$222 billion, pacing down 10.3%.

A separate data set from PEI Group shows that mega-funds holding final closes in the first three quarters of 2026 gathered US$169.5 billion, or 24% of all private equity capital raised. Data providers count funds differently, so these figures are best read side by side and not combined.

New managers have felt this most. PitchBook reports that only 36 first-time private equity funds had closed by the middle of 2026, raising US$7.7 billion between them. Its chart for 2021 shows 297 first-time funds closing that year.

Why allocators are concentrating

PitchBook reads the trend as allocators "gravitating toward GPs with established track records and broad market validation rather than expending the resources required to underwrite smaller, less-proven vehicles." It also points to two pressures on the allocator side. Many institutions are close to their limits for private markets, and distributions have been slow. PitchBook estimates that almost US$5 trillion of capital sits in funds seven years or older, 39% of total net asset value at the end of 2025.

An allocator waiting for cash to come back from older funds has less to commit to new ones. When it does commit, the simplest decision is to re-up with a manager it already holds. Underwriting a new relationship takes staff and committee time, and both are limited.

PitchBook adds one line that matters for mid-size managers in particular. The middle market, it says, "is struggling to elbow its way into new allocations, even if it is winning on performance." Performance is necessary. On its own, it does not secure a place in the queue.

A smaller fund faces the same committee questions as a large one, with less room for error.

What this means for a first-time or mid-size manager

None of this closes the market to smaller funds. Capital is still being committed, and allocators still need managers outside the largest platforms for specific exposures. What changes is the standard a smaller raise has to meet before an allocator spends time on it. In our experience, four points carry the most weight.

A specific reason to be in the portfolio. An allocator that already holds several large buyout funds does not need another broad one. A smaller manager has to show what it gives the portfolio that the large platforms do not, whether that is a sector, a deal size, a region or a source of transactions. That case has to be stated plainly in the first pages of the materials and tested against the allocator's existing holdings.

A realised record, attributed to the people in the room. When distributions are slow, cash returned carries more weight than unrealised marks. Committees separate the two and ask who was responsible for each result. A first-time team spun out of a larger firm should expect close questions on attribution, and should have the deal-level data ready before the first meeting.

A fund size the allocator can believe. A target set above what the strategy and the investor base can support tends to show in the first few meetings. A realistic target, with a hard cap that matches the opportunity, reads as discipline. It also makes the first close easier to reach, which matters because allocators look at who has already committed.

A longer plan, and a deeper list. With fewer allocators making new commitments, and each one taking longer, a raise needs more names at the start and a schedule that assumes the process will take time. A list built only from the allocators who have backed similar funds before is likely to be the same list every other manager in the category is using.

A note on the wealth channel

PitchBook also reports that US evergreen private equity assets nearly doubled from US$51.3 billion at the end of 2024 to US$99.3 billion in the first quarter of 2026. It notes that this money tends to flow to the largest managers, which already have wealth products in place. For most first-time and mid-size funds, institutional and family office capital remains the core of the raise.

Our view

Concentration reflects allocators conserving time. They back the managers they already know because it is the decision with the fewest unknowns. A smaller manager raising into that market needs to remove unknowns before the first meeting. In practice that comes down to the four points above, which are a clear case for its place in the portfolio, a record that answers the attribution question, a target that holds up when tested and a list of allocators broad enough to absorb a longer process.
