After the first close: why raises slow down, and what keeps them moving

A first close is a milestone, and it is also the point at which many raises lose pace. The reasons recur from one raise to the next, and most of them can be seen, and addressed, before the first close is held.

A first close is the moment a fund can begin investing. It shows that a group of investors has committed, and it gives the manager something to point to with the allocators who have not. It is also, in many raises, the point at which pace drops. Commitments that seemed close take longer, and the time between the first and final close stretches toward its limit.

Our analysis of open private raises gives a sense of how few raises reach the later stages. Of 951 open offerings of US$50 million or more, 253 had sold less than a quarter of their target on their latest filing, 85 had sold between a quarter and a half, and 46 had sold more than half. The number falls at each step. The filings do not say why. Across the raises we see, the same patterns account for most of the slowdown.

The first close used the warmest capital

Most first closes are built from the investors closest to the manager, such as existing investors re-upping and the partners' own commitment. That is the right capital to start with. It also means that the allocators left after the first close are, almost by definition, the ones who needed more convincing.

When the institutional part of the list was not worked in parallel with the first close, the manager starts that work from the beginning at the point when it expected to be finishing. The calendar of an investment committee does not compress to match. The raises that keep moving after a first close are usually the ones where institutional conversations started months before it, so that the first close arrives as news to allocators already in diligence.

The manager's attention moves to investing

Once a fund has held a first close, it can deploy capital, and it usually should. Deals that were waiting for the fund begin to move. The senior team that ran the raise is now also running transactions, and the raise gets less of their time.

Allocators notice. Slower replies and meetings moved at short notice are read as a signal about how the manager will treat investors after the commitment. A raise that is to keep pace after the first close needs a clear owner whose time is not taken up by the deals.

A first close proves the raise can start. The work that follows decides whether it finishes.

Nothing new to say

An allocator that passed or deferred before the first close needs a reason to look again. A larger commitment total is one reason, though often not enough by itself. What tends to move a later allocator is new information about the strategy in practice. That can be the first investments made from the fund or progress on realisations in earlier funds.

Raises that slow after a first close often have no planned updates for the period that follows. The manager returns to the same allocators with the same materials and a higher commitment figure. The raises that keep moving plan the updates in advance, so that each return to an allocator brings something it has not yet seen.

Terms that work against later investors

Anchor and early investors often receive better terms in return for committing first, through fee discounts or other rights in side letters. Those terms are reasonable and common. Later investors, though, will read them, and a most favoured nation clause can extend some of them further than intended.

Later investors also bear the cost of coming in after the first close. Fund documents commonly require them to pay their share of earlier capital calls, with an equalisation amount on top, and they commonly limit how long after the first close new investors can be admitted. If the economics for a later investor look worse than those offered to early ones, without a clear reason, the raise slows. Setting the structure of early-closer terms with the whole raise in mind avoids most of this.

The fund size stops matching the demand

Some raises set a target that the first close shows to be ambitious. The manager then faces a choice between holding the target and accepting a long tail, or adjusting the expectation. Allocators who are watching will read either decision. A target revised with a clear explanation tends to be read as discipline. A raise that keeps extending without explanation is read as one that has stalled.

What we take from it

The pace of a raise after its first close is mostly set before it. The institutional pipeline, the updates planned for later allocators, the structure of early terms and a fund size the market supports are all decided early. On a mandate, we plan the second half of the raise at the same time as the first, so that a first close becomes a point of momentum for the allocators still deciding.
