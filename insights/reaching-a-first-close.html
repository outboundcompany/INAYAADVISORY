What separates the raises that reach a first close

Most open private raises in our data have not yet reported a first sale, and allocators rarely want to be the first to commit. The raises that do reach a first close tend to share a small set of features, and almost all of them are in place before the first meeting.

The first commitment is the hardest one in any raise. Allocators look at who else has committed, and a raise with nothing committed asks each of them to go first. Our analysis of open private raises found that 567 of 951 offerings of $50 million or more reported nothing sold on their latest filing. Six in ten had not yet recorded a first sale.

The filings do not explain why. Across the raises we see, the ones that reach a first close tend to have the same features in place, and most of those features are decided before the raise goes to market.

A first close with a date and a figure

Raises that reach a first close usually name one. They set a date and a minimum amount for it, and they tell allocators both. That gives every conversation a reason to reach a decision by a certain point.

A raise without a stated first close leaves each allocator free to wait and see who commits. Most will. A named date does not force anyone to commit, though it does change the question from "whether" to "by when", and it gives the manager a clear test of which conversations are moving.

The figure matters as much as the date. A first close set at a level the manager can reach with its closest investors and one or two institutions is achievable. A first close set at a large share of the target can turn into a second target that the raise also misses.

An anchor, or a reason to go first

Most first closes are built around one or more investors who commit before the others. Sometimes that is an existing investor re-upping. Sometimes it is a new institution that receives a benefit for going first, such as a reduced fee or a co-investment right. Arrangements of this kind are common, and they are disclosed to later investors.

Raises that reach a first close have usually identified who that investor might be before launch, and have opened the conversation early. Raises that wait for an anchor to appear from the general process tend to wait for a long time.

Allocators look for who else is in. The first commitment has to be earned before anyone else will follow.

The manager's own commitment

Allocators read the general partner's commitment as a statement about conviction. A commitment that is meaningful relative to the partners' own means, and paid in cash, answers a question before it is asked. It also counts toward the first close. Raises where the general partner commitment is small or funded through fee waivers tend to face that question in every meeting.

Ready for diligence on day one

An allocator that is interested will ask for the data room. If it is not ready, or if it is incomplete, the interest cools while the manager catches up. The raises that reach a first close have the full set of materials in place before the first meeting. That covers the track record at deal level, the fund documents in draft, the operational and compliance policies, and references.

The same applies to the questions behind them. An investment committee comes to a raise with a familiar set, covering team and continuity, track record and attribution, strategy, terms, pipeline and operations. Materials that answer those questions in writing let the allocator's staff take the fund to committee without coming back for more.

Something to invest in

A raise with a visible pipeline gives an allocator a reason to commit now. When the early transactions are identified and well advanced, the allocator can see where its money will go first and how quickly. Some managers go further and hold early assets in advance of the first close, which shows the strategy at work before the fund exists.

A raise with a general description of the opportunity and no named transactions asks the allocator to take the deployment plan on trust. That is harder at the first close than at any later point.

What we take from it

None of these features is unusual. Each is well known to anyone who has raised a fund. What separates the raises that reach a first close is that all of them are in place at once, before the first allocator meeting. On a mandate, that preparation is where most of our early work goes, in the materials, the terms and the order in which allocators see the raise.
