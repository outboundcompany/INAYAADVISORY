The First Close

The point at which a raise becomes a fund or a company with committed capital. Most raises stop before they reach it.

How we work

Introduction to the first close

The first close, in brief

  1. 01

    Most open raises have sold nothing

    567 of 951 open offerings of US$50 million or more reported nothing sold on their latest filing.

  2. 02

    Allocators look at who else is in

    A raise with nothing committed asks each allocator to go first. Most wait to see who does.

  3. 03

    The raises that close name one

    A stated date and minimum amount give every allocator a point by which to decide.

What is a first close?

Until the first commitment arrives, each allocator is asked to go first, and most wait to see who does. The first close is the point at which that changes.

The filings do not explain why most raises stall. Across the raises we see, the ones that reach a first close tend to have the same features in place, and most of them are decided before the raise goes to market.

Note: SEC EDGAR Form D filings, twelve months to July 2026, Inaya Advisory analysis. Amounts are as reported on each issuer's most recent Form D and can lag what has been raised. For information only.

How far have open raises got?

Most of these raises have made little progress on paper. 567 of the 951 reported nothing sold. A further 253 had sold less than a quarter of their target. Only 46 had sold more than half.

Source: SEC EDGAR Form D filings, twelve months to July 2026. Inaya Advisory analysis.

How much is still to be raised?

Together the 951 offerings sought US$149.3 billion. On their most recent filings, US$129.8 billion of that is still to be raised.

Note: SEC EDGAR Form D filings, twelve months to July 2026, Inaya Advisory analysis. For information only.

What the raises that close have in place

Most of these are decided before the raise goes to market.

  • Materials

    Materials that answer the committee before the first meeting, so the meeting goes to the decision.

  • Sequence

    A considered order, starting with the allocators most likely to commit early.

  • Introductions

    An introduction from someone the allocator already knows is read and answered.

  • An anchor

    One or more investors who commit before the others and give the rest a reason to follow.

  • Disclosed terms

    Any benefit for committing early is disclosed to the investors who follow.

How we work

Preparing a raise for its first close

Before launch

  • Materials that answer the committee before the first meeting
  • A first close date and a figure the raise can reach
  • One or more investors identified to commit first

During the raise

  • A considered order, starting with the allocators most likely to commit early
  • Diligence and commitment conversations carried through until terms are agreed

Anchor investors who know us

We hold at least one standing investor relationship for every kind of mandate we take, so the first conversations of a raise are with investors who expect to hear from us.

A raise without a first close

Of 951 private offerings of US$50 million or more open in the twelve months to July 2026, 567 reported nothing sold and US$129.8 billion was still to be raised. A raise that reports nothing sold has not reached its first close.

Reviewed before the date is set

The first conversation is with Abbas Chothia, our Client Advisor, and is held in confidence. David Hunter, Senior Advisor, and Anas Ansar, Managing Partner, then review the raise, and the principal has our answer before any date is set.

Source: SEC EDGAR Form D filings, twelve months to July 2026. Inaya Advisory analysis, published in Open private raises.

Before setting a first close

The figure matters as much as the date. A first close the manager can reach with its closest investors and one or two institutions is achievable. One set at a large share of the target can turn into a second target that the raise also misses.

Frequently asked questions

What is a first close?

The point at which a raise becomes a fund or a company with committed capital. Most raises stop before they reach it.

Why do allocators wait?

Allocators look at who else has committed. A raise with nothing committed asks each of them to go first, and most will wait to see who does.

What is an anchor investor?

One or more investors who commit before the others. Sometimes that is an existing investor re-upping. Sometimes it is a new institution that receives a benefit for going first, which is disclosed to later investors.

Why name a date and a figure?

A named date gives every conversation a point by which to decide. The figure should be one the raise can reach with its closest investors and one or two institutions.
